Tutorial 09 · Using your model

Detecting spindles in your own data

This tutorial takes you from an EDF file recorded in your lab to a table of detected spindles: checking that your data resemble the training data, exporting one channel, reproducing the Portiloop preprocessing (in MATLAB or Python), running the pre-trained network, turning its output into events, and checking that the result is physiologically plausible. You can also try the detector on your own file directly in this page.

In this tutorial you will

  • Check whether your montage, population and sleep stages match what the model was trained on
  • Export one EEG channel from EDF with pyedflib (or MNE)
  • Preprocess it exactly like the Portiloop, with the MATLAB script or a tested Python equivalent
  • Run the pre-trained network with run_offline_unlabelled and understand its output
  • Convert probabilities into spindle events and sanity-check density, duration and frequency

Will the model work on my data?

The pre-trained network was trained on a narrow, well-defined kind of data. The closer your recordings are to it, the more you can trust the detections. The repository's README is explicit: "note that the data must be collected in the same experimental setting as MODA for this to work".

What the network was trained on

The labels come from the MODA project (Massive Online Data Annotation), whose gold-standard spindles were scored by a median of 5 experts on recordings of the Montreal Archive of Sleep Studies (MASS) [2, 3]. From the MODA paper's methods [2]:

  • "180 subjects were sampled from the SS1-SS5 subsets": 100 younger adults (phase 1, mean age 24.1 years) and 80 older adults (phase 2, mean age 62.0 years).
  • "“Blocks” of 115 s were randomly extracted from artifact free Stage N2 sleep." Three blocks (about 6 minutes) per subject for most subjects, 10 blocks for 15 subjects of each phase.
  • "C3 channel was reformatted to C3-A2 when possible otherwise the original reference “C3-Linked Ear” (C3-LE) was kept."

The authors' training files contained 162 of these subjects (98 from phase 1, 64 from phase 2). They are not distributed with these tutorials: the practice dataset of Tutorial 02 is synthetic, and Tutorial 02 lists real annotated databases you can request.

The dataset generator of this repository follows the same rules: it reads the C3 channel of each MASS EDF file, subtracts A2 for the subjects listed with a C3-A2 montage in MODA's channel table, and checks that the original sampling rate is 256 Hz (resampled to 250 Hz afterwards):

Be honest about generalizationThe Portiloop paper itself lists this limitation: "all our experiments are based on the MODA dataset rather than actual nights spent wearing the Portiloop device", and recommends caution "because the EEG acquisition and signal may differ somewhat from the training data or between individuals" [1]. It also notes that "The MODA dataset is relatively small (∼24 h of annotated data) and heterogeneous." If your montage, population or hardware differs, hand-score a few minutes of N2 from several participants and compute precision and recall as in Tutorial 06 before you interpret detections.

How the widget differs from the Python pipeline
  • Resampling: a windowed-sinc (band-limited) resampler written for this page. On our test signals at 100, 256, 500 and 1000 Hz, it differed from scipy.signal.resample_poly by 0.1 to 0.3% (relative RMS), and the standardized signals by at most 0.005. The library's PL.resampleLinear, which is didactic only, differed by 20% from 100 Hz and 3% from 256 Hz, which is why it is not used here.
  • Network stride: to stay fast, the page runs the network every 5 samples with 8 interleaved hidden states (like the real-time simulation of Tutorial 08), whereas run_offline_unlabelled runs it at every sample with 42 hidden states. On a 2-minute test, the two outputs differed by 0.01 on average (maximum 0.27), and 27 of 5500 compared positions fell on different sides of a 0.5 threshold.
  • Length: the first 10 minutes only (about 35 to 40 s of computation on our laptop).
  • End result: on the 10-minute test file of the Python walkthrough, the code of this widget (run with Node.js) found 64 events and the Python pipeline 64; all matched, with a median onset difference of 8 ms.
  • Timing: event times are corrected for the 40 ms filter delay and refer to the start of your file; detections in the first 15 s (warm-up) are discarded.

Step by step, with the Python package

For whole nights, many participants, or analyses you will publish, use the Python pipeline. The five steps below were run end to end on a synthetic 10-minute EDF file (C3 and A2 channels at 256 Hz, 66 spindles); the outputs shown are from that test.

  1. Export one EEG channel to a text file. pyedflib is installed with the package (it is listed in setup.py). Adapt the file name and the channel labels: print the labels first, since every system names them differently (C3-M2, EEG C3-A2, C3...). If the C3 channel is already referenced to A2 or M2, do not subtract anything. 
    import numpy as np
import pyedflib

edf_path = "night1.edf"
with pyedflib.EdfReader(edf_path) as edf:
    labels = edf.getSignalLabels()
    print("channels:", labels)
    i_c3 = [i for i, s in enumerate(labels) if "C3" in s][0]
    i_a2 = [i for i, s in enumerate(labels) if "A2" in s][0]
    fs = edf.getSampleFrequency(i_c3)
    c3 = edf.readSignal(i_c3)              # physical units, usually microvolts
    a2 = edf.readSignal(i_a2)
    assert edf.getSampleFrequency(i_a2) == fs
eeg = c3 - a2                              # C3-A2, as MODA used when possible
np.savetxt("night1_C3-A2.txt", eeg, fmt="%.6g")
print(f"{len(eeg)} samples at {fs} Hz = {len(eeg) / fs / 60:.1f} min")

  2. Preprocess like the Portiloop. Choose one of the two options; they apply the same filters and the same standardization.

    Option A: the MATLAB script

    Open portiloop_software/matlab/preprocess_unannotated_data.m and edit its first lines (explained line by line in Tutorial 03):

    path = "/data/mylab/exports/";                  % folder that contains your text file (keep the final slash)

    Then run it from the portiloop_software/matlab folder (so that Simulink finds filter_lp.slx). It needs Simulink, the Signal Processing Toolbox and the DSP System Toolbox. It writes preprocessed_night1_C3-A2.txt next to your input file. A whole night may take a while in Simulink.

    Validation and remaining differences
    • Python versus the JavaScript of these pages: on 10 minutes of synthetic EEG at 250 Hz with 60 Hz noise, a DC offset and an electrode step, the Python output and PL.preprocess (in docs/assets/js/portiloop.js, run with Node.js) differed by at most 7 × 10−14 (float64 rounding); after writing to text in single precision, by 5 × 10−6.
    • JavaScript network versus PyTorch: on 2 minutes of preprocessed signal, the JavaScript network run at every sample with 42 hidden states reproduced all 29,946 outputs of run_offline_unlabelled to within 1.7 × 10−6, with no threshold crossing changed.
    • Python versus MATLAB: not compared numerically (we did not run MATLAB). By construction, two differences remain: (1) the MATLAB script removes the FIR delay offline (use --compensate-delay to do the same; set FIR_DELAY = 0 in step 4 in that case), and pads the end by repeating its last output samples; (2) MATLAB's resample and SciPy's resample_poly both use Kaiser-windowed anti-aliasing filters, but with slightly different designs, so resampled values differ slightly.
  3. Run the network. The package exposes run_offline_unlabelled(config_dict, path_experiments, unlabelled_segment), which loads a checkpoint and runs it over a whole preprocessed signal. This is what notebooks/offline_inference.ipynb does. 
    import numpy as np
import torch
from pathlib import Path
import portiloop_software
from portiloop_software import run_offline_unlabelled, get_final_model_config_dict

config_dict = get_final_model_config_dict()      # the published model (checkpoint pareto_search_15_35_v4_0)
path_experiments = Path(portiloop_software.__file__).parent / "experiments"

data = np.loadtxt("preprocessed_night1_C3-A2.txt", dtype=np.float32)
output, true_idx = run_offline_unlabelled(config_dict=config_dict,
                                          path_experiments=path_experiments,
                                          unlabelled_segment=torch.from_numpy(data))
prob = output.numpy()          # one probability per position, between 0 and 1
idx = true_idx.numpy()         # index of the last (most recent) sample of each 54-sample window
print(len(data), "samples ->", len(prob), "outputs, first index", idx[0], "last index", idx[-1])
np.savetxt("night1_probabilities.csv", np.c_[idx, prob], delimiter=",",
           fmt=["%d", "%.5f"], header="sample_index,probability", comments="")

    What the outputs are. Reading run_offline_unlabelled and the helpers it calls:

    • The parameters come from the configuration dictionary: window int(0.218 × 250) = 54 samples, seq_stride = int(0.170 × 250) = 42 samples, and validation_network_stride = 1, so the network is evaluated at every sample.
    • UnlabelledSignalDatasetSingleSegment returns single windows full_signal[i:i + 54] (starting at i = 1) together with the index of the window's most recent sample, the same convention as the training labels: 
              signal_seq = self.full_signal[idx:idx + self.window_size].unfold(0, self.window_size, 1)
        true_idx = idx + self.window_size - 1  # index of the last (most recent) sample of the window, as for training labels
        return signal_seq, true_idx
      The first output is therefore at index 54, and its probability refers to samples 1 to 54: it is what the network says once it has seen sample 54.
    • ValidationSampler arranges the windows in batches of 42 consecutive positions, and the GRU hidden state of batch element j is carried to element j of the next batch, i.e. to the window 42 samples later. In other words, 42 interleaved hidden states each see windows 42 samples (168 ms) apart, the same spacing as the training sequences.
    • The number of outputs is 42 × floor((N − 55) / 42), a whole number of batches: the last few samples (at most 42) get no output.
    Which checkpoint is loadedget_final_model_config_dict() names the experiment 'sanity_check_final_model_0'. run_offline_unlabelled loads the network through get_trained_model, which falls back to portiloop_software/experiments/pareto_search_15_35_v4_0 when no file of that name exists, so the published model is used. In older clones of the repository, run_offline_unlabelled did not have this fallback and raised FileNotFoundError: ... sanity_check_final_model_0; run git pull, or add config_dict["experiment_name"] = "pareto_search_15_35_v4_0" after creating the dictionary.
    Older clones of the repositoryBefore the current version, run_offline_unlabelled had three problems that are now fixed: it did not find the default checkpoint (see above); it raised AssertionError: Index out of range whenever (N − 54) was a multiple of 42 (for example exactly 2 minutes, 30000 samples); and its true_idx pointed one sample after each window. If you see the assertion error or a first index of 55 instead of 54, run git pull (and reinstall with pip install -e . if needed).
    On the hard drive
    • Reads: the preprocessed text file; the checkpoint portiloop_software/experiments/pareto_search_15_35_v4_0 (about 300 KB, included in the repository; nine other checkpoints _3 to _18 sit next to it).
    • Writes: night1_probabilities.csv with a header and two columns, sample_index (at 250 Hz, in the preprocessed file) and probability.
  4. Threshold the output and convert it to events. A spindle event is a run of consecutive positions where the probability is at or above a threshold. Three choices shape the result:
    • Threshold (default 0.5, as in the notebook): higher means fewer, more certain detections. Choose it on hand-scored data from your own lab (Tutorial 06).
    • Minimum duration (default 0.3 s): very short runs are usually noise. MODA removed gold-standard events "longer than 2.5 s or shorter than 0.3 s" [2].
    • Merge gap (default 0.1 s): two runs separated by a brief dip are counted as one spindle.
    The function also removes the 10-sample filter delay (if you used the causal Python preprocessing) and drops detections in the first 15 s (warm-up). 
    import numpy as np

FS = 250
FIR_DELAY = 10   # samples; set to 0 if you preprocessed with --compensate-delay or with the MATLAB script

def probabilities_to_events(idx, prob, threshold=0.5, min_duration_s=0.3, merge_gap_s=0.1,
                            warmup_s=15.0, fs=FS, delay=FIR_DELAY):
    """Threshold the network output; return a list of (onset_s, offset_s, peak_probability)."""
    above = prob >= threshold
    edges = np.diff(np.concatenate([[0], above.astype(int), [0]]))
    starts, stops = np.flatnonzero(edges == 1), np.flatnonzero(edges == -1)   # stops are exclusive
    events = []
    for a, b in zip(starts, stops):
        onset, offset = idx[a] - delay, idx[b - 1] + 1 - delay              # in samples, offset exclusive
        peak = float(prob[a:b].max())
        if events and onset - events[-1][1] <= merge_gap_s * fs:          # close to the previous one: merge
            events[-1] = (events[-1][0], offset, max(events[-1][2], peak))
        else:
            events.append((onset, offset, peak))
    return [(on / fs, off / fs, p) for on, off, p in events
            if off - on >= min_duration_s * fs and on >= warmup_s * fs]

table = np.loadtxt("night1_probabilities.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1)
idx, prob = table[:, 0].astype(int), table[:, 1]
events = probabilities_to_events(idx, prob, threshold=0.5)
with open("night1_spindles.csv", "w") as fh:
    fh.write("onset_s,offset_s,duration_s,peak_probability\n")
    for on, off, p in events:
        fh.write(f"{on:.3f},{off:.3f},{off - on:.3f},{p:.3f}\n")
print(len(events), "events written to night1_spindles.csv")

    For neuroscientists: detected boundaries are lateAn event starts when the network has seen enough of the spindle to be confident, not at the physiological onset. The paper reports a detection delay of the network of about 250 ms (±100 ms) on top of the fixed delays [1]; on our synthetic test file, detected onsets were a median 0.32 s after the true onsets. Use the events to count spindles and to locate them; if you need precise onsets, durations or amplitudes, re-measure them on the raw signal around each detection (for example with a sigma-band envelope).
  5. Sanity-check the result. A detector can fail silently on data unlike its training set. Compare three simple statistics with published values. 
    import numpy as np

FS = 250
ev = np.loadtxt("night1_spindles.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, ndmin=2)
eeg = np.loadtxt("preprocessed_night1_C3-A2.txt")
minutes = (len(eeg) / FS - 15) / 60                  # exclude the 15 s warm-up
dur = ev[:, 2]
print(f"{len(ev)} spindles in {minutes:.1f} min -> {len(ev) / minutes:.2f} per minute")
print(f"duration: median {np.median(dur):.2f} s, 10th to 90th percentile "
      f"{np.percentile(dur, 10):.2f} to {np.percentile(dur, 90):.2f} s")

# dominant frequency inside each event (zero-padded spectrum of the preprocessed EEG)
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(1024, 1 / FS)
band = (f >= 5) & (f <= 25)
peaks = []
for on, off in ev[:, :2]:
    seg = eeg[int(on * FS):int(off * FS)]
    seg = (seg - seg.mean()) * np.hanning(len(seg))
    peaks.append(f[band][np.argmax(np.abs(np.fft.rfft(seg, 1024))[band])])
peaks = np.array(peaks)
inside = np.mean((peaks >= 11) & (peaks <= 16)) * 100
print(f"peak frequency: median {np.median(peaks):.1f} Hz; {inside:.0f}% of events between 11 and 16 Hz")

    Density is only meaningful per minute of N2. If you have a hypnogram with one label per epoch, keep the events that fall in N2 epochs: 
    import numpy as np

EPOCH_S = 30                                              # 30 s epochs (AASM); use 20 for R&K-scored files

ev = np.loadtxt("night1_spindles.csv", delimiter=",", skiprows=1, ndmin=2)

print(f"{in_n2.sum()} of {len(ev)} events fall in N2; N2 density {in_n2.sum() / n2_minutes:.2f} per minute")

    What to compare with. The MODA gold standard, i.e. the very data the network learned from, reports per-subject medians (standard deviation across subjects) [2]:

    MODA gold standard (C3, N2)Younger adults (phase 1)Older adults (phase 2)
    Density (spindles per minute)4.2 (2.4)2.6 (2.2)

    The AASM definition quoted by MODA describes a spindle as a train of distinct waves at 11 to 16 Hz lasting at least 0.5 s [2]. Red flags: a density far above 10 per minute or near 0 in N2; many events in wake or REM; median durations under 0.4 s or over 1.5 s; most peak frequencies outside 11 to 16 Hz (alpha around 8 to 12 Hz in wake, or muscle artifacts). Always look at a random sample of 20 to 30 detections in your EEG viewer.

Density at different thresholds

In the first widget, use the demo recording. Note the number of detected spindles and the density at thresholds 0.3, 0.5 and 0.8. Then compare with the number of true spindles (purple bars after 15 s). Which threshold gives the most plausible count, and what does this tell you about reporting spindle density from an automatic detector?

Answer

The count changes with the threshold (lower thresholds add short or doubtful events, higher ones miss weak spindles), so "spindle density" is partly a property of the detector settings. Always report the threshold, minimum duration and merge gap, use the same settings for all participants and conditions, and, ideally, calibrate the threshold on hand-scored data from your own lab (Tutorial 06).

Which file did the network really see?

You preprocessed one recording with the MATLAB script and another with the Python script (default options), then ran events.py unchanged on both. Are the event times of the two recordings comparable?

Answer

Not exactly: the MATLAB output is already shifted 40 ms earlier, so with FIR_DELAY = 10 its events end up 40 ms too early. Set FIR_DELAY = 0 for files preprocessed with MATLAB or with --compensate-delay. The detections themselves (which spindles are found) are the same, because the network sees the same waveforms.

What next

To measure how good these detections are on your data, hand-score a subset and follow Tutorial 06. If performance is poor on your montage or population, consider retraining (Tutorial 05). To use the network for real-time stimulation, see Tutorial 08 and Tutorial 10.
